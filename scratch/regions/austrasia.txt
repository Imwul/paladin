As the Ardennes is the home for your first knights, we have 
provided much more detail here than for other areas. 
The Ardennes Forest
Long ago, the vast and majestic forest known as Arduenna 
Silva was the domain of the goddess Arduinna, a huntress sim -
ilar to Diana, who was pictured as riding a boar and wielding a 
hunter’s knife. She was worshiped for her protective and healing 
powers. When the region was evangelized by St. W alfroy, and 
later St. Remaclus, all pagan sanctuaries and sites dedicated to 
Arduinna were destroyed, exorcized or converted.
The borders of the duchy of Ardennes are roughly drawn 
in the west by the Meuse river, in the south by the line along the 
Semois via Arlon to T rier, in the east along the ancient Roman 
road leading from T rier to Cologne, and in the north from the 
city of Dinant to the castle of Franchimont and from there to the 
eastward Roman crossroads at Dahlem.
The main feature of the Ardennes’ geography is its steep 
rocky hills, almost entirely covered by an immense primeval 
forest like a green ocean of trees. The uninhabited wood with

The word Ardennes itself is said to mean Bitter W oods 
and, indeed, this inhospitable and dark forest is an enchanted 
place containing many rocks and ravines, hilly pine wastes of 
charcoal makers, “a place of horror and solitary isolation which 
abounds with wild beasts. ”  Apart from the occasional bears, 
boars, wolves and other wild animals, travelers may have to 
face faeries (the woods between La Roche and Bastogne are 
known as Fays Forest), nutons (a local variant of dwarfs) and 
other such marvelous creatures like the luring will-o-wisps, 
hungry giants or, like Aubery the Burgundian, even a forest 
lion or a lonely dragon.
Settlements
Apart from Bastogne, which lies on an important crossroad, 
the main towns are situated around the outside, not actually 
inside of the forest. The inner forest is almost com -
pletely free of habitation. A few cultivated areas 
exist in clearings, but the only other habitations are 
the villages and royal palaces in the river valleys and 
relays along the Romans roads.
Politics
The duchy of the Ardennes is ruled by Duke 
Thierry. His vast domain is divided into five coun -
ties: Arlon, Bastogne, Bouillon, La Roche and 
Salm. Duke Thierry is the Count of Bastogne, and 
the four remaining counties are governed by Count 
Galeran of Arlon, Count Aymon of Bouillon, 
Count Hugo of La Roche and Count Widogast 
of Salm. The duke’s military power extends to the 
surrounding abbeys of Stavelot-Malmédy, Prüm, 
and Saint Hubert. Even though these rich eccle -
siastical domains are politically independent — 
called immunities — they serve under the duke’s 
banner in times of war. The spiritual power over 
the Ardennes is wielded by the very influential 
prince-bishop of Liege.
Travel
Roman roads have been partially preserved 
from Cologne to Amblève and Bastogne, leading 
further south to Reims; from T ongres to Bastogne 
and Arlon, leading to Metz; from Bastogne west -
ward to Dinant; and from Cologne to Dinant.
Royal relays are established along these roads 
every 20 miles or so. These consist of a village or an 
isolated palace where messengers can obtain fresh 
horses. Within the forest, such relays are often the 
only inhabited places for miles around.
The duchy’s western frontier is formed by a natural barrier, 
the Meuse. This great navigable river is a genuine aquatic high -
way, with such commercial river ports as Dinant, Namur, Huy, 
Liege and Maastricht.
Economy and Trade
The duchy generates much income from road and bridge 
tolls. The main agricultural activities are sheep herding and 
farming. Hunting is a significant source of wealth, as it pro -
vides many households with fresh meat and leather. All no -
bles sacrifice their first kill of the season to St. Hubert, or 
otherwise offer gifts to the local saint, in order to attract his 
benevolence.
The forest contains a number of iron, copper, lead, zinc 
and even gold mines, giving rise to metalworking industries 
in a few towns. Many stone quarries produce good building 
materials. Most commercial activities are limited to local 
markets, while long-distance trade in swords and mill stones 
is in the hands of Lombard merchants who have installed 
themselves at Bastogne.

knight, he is loyal to the death and renowned for the expert 
hunting parties he frequently organizes on his domains, often in

The House of Clermont
The families of Thierry’s uncles Aymon, Bevis and Doon of 
Rethel, are considered a separate and independent House. These 
three Ardennes lords consider their Ardennes domains as allods. 
Aymon: The sixth of Doon of Mayence’s twelve sons is 
vengeful and proud, but unquestionably courageous. The Count 
of Bouillon, he lives in the citadel of Bouillon and is married 
to Adelaid, a modest, forgiving and merciful daughter of King 
Pepin and the false Queen Aliste, and therefore Charlemagne’s 
half-sister, until Adelaid’s death in 756. Their children are 
Renaud, Alard, and Richard. Aymon is now married to Beatrice 
of Clermont, with whom he has twins, a boy and girl: Guido the 
Wild and Bradamant.
Bevis of Aigremont: Aymon’s younger brother has a dark 
streak of vengefulness and pride and is nicknamed “the Beardless.” 
He is the father of the twins Maugis and Vivien of Monbranc. 
Maugis is a generous and loyal knight, and a wily enchanter who 
was raised by faeries. His twin brother Vivien was abducted at 
birth and taken to Spain to be raised as a Moor. He fought against 
his own father, but was captured and converted by Maugis in 766.
Doon of Rethel: The eleventh of Doon of Mayence’s sons 
is an energetic, generous and indulgent count, who, strangely 
enough, has six fingers on each hand.
The House of La Roche
Doon of La Roche: The glorious and venerable Count of 
Cologne, Princess Olive’s husband. His eyes still sparkle with the

Hugo of La Roche: Duke Thierry’s eldest half-brother is
