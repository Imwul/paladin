T
he Carolingian epics are peopled by heroes and villains, 
knights and ladies, paladins and wicked pagans. Below 
are statistics for some of the major characters of the epics, 
and lists of many of the “big names” of the epics. 
Charlemagne (736–814)
Powerful, prudent, wise, modest,
Illustrious, affable, learned, good, able, upright,
Gentle, distinguished, just, pious, an outstanding hero,
A king and a ruler, held in the highest respect, august, 
excellent,
A distinguished arbiter and judge, compassionate to the 
poor,
Peaceful, generous, skillful, good-spirited, handsome.
— Einhard
T
hese laudatory verses were written by one of the court -
iers, Einhard, to describe Charlemagne. Indeed, the glo -
rious leader of the Franks is the perfect embodiment of a 
king and emperor, divinely inspired and guided in his mission to 
build a solid Christian realm: 
Charlemagne practiced the Christian religion with great 
devotion and piety. As long as he lived he went to church 
morning and evening with great regularity.
— Einhard
Partly because of this, he is an enlightened king, who spends 
personal money for the common good and his people, by fund -
ing the acquisition of grain, and paying for the construction of 
canals, bridges and roads. And as all Frankish kings, he distrib -
utes his wealth generously among those loyal to him: 
He was most active in relieving the poor and in that form 
of really disinterested charity which the Greeks call elee-
mosyna. He gave alms not only in his own country, but 
also across the sea. Wherever he heard that Christians 
were living in want, he took pity on their poverty and 
sent them money regularly.
— Einhard

Charlemagne is famous for his inexhaustible energy: 
Charlemagne, who could never endure idleness and sloth, 
prepared to go off into the forest grove to hunt aurochs.
— Notker
He is naturally good-humored, very sincere and unusually 
temperate (except for red meat): 
He was moderate in his eating and drinking, and espe -
cially so in drinking; for he hated to see drunkenness in 
any man, and even more so in himself and his friends. 
[…] He was so sparing in his use of wine and every other 
beverage that he rarely drank more than three times in 
the course of his dinner. 
— Einhard
During his life the king has many women and concubines. 
However, he never commits adultery. His only noteworthy char-
acter flaws are his tendency to act stubbornly, and to not easily 
forgive traitors: 
The sons of two nobles, whose duty it was to guard the en-
trance to the King’s tent, lay there dead to the world one 
night, as a result of the drink they had guzzled. Char -
lemagne, who, according to his custom, was unceasingly 
vigilant, went the rounds of his camp and then quietly re-
turned to his tent without anybody recognizing him. The 
next morning he called all the leaders of the realm to him 
and asked what punishment ought to be meted out to a 
man who betrayed the king into the hands of the enemy. 
The two nobles whom I mentioned in the beginning were 
completely unaware of what had happened, and they 
proffered it as their opinion that such a man should be 
condemned to death. Charlemagne gave the young men a 
wigging and then let them go unharmed.
— Notker
As the leader of his people, he subscribes to the traditional 
values of family life and hospitality, and is preoccupied with his 
reputation and especially with God’s precepts: 
He loved foreigners and took great pains to make them wel-
come. So many visited him as a result that they were rightly

He spent much time on horseback and out hunting, which

He took delight in steam-baths at thermal springs, and

The king is an excellent warrior, but also a man who has re-
ceived a solid education: he speaks Frankish and Latin, and even 
understands Greek. Being eloquent himself, he has a particular love 
for arts, especially stories and music. A good song recited at the right 
moment can make him change his mind on important matters: 
He spoke easily and fluently, and could express with great clar-
ity whatever he had to say. He learned Latin so well he spoke it 
as fluently as his own mother tongue; but he understood Greek 
better than he spoke it. (…) Under Alcuin, the king spent much 
time studying rhetoric, dialectic and especially astrology. He 
also tried to learn to write, but despite his best efforts he had 
begun too late in life and made little progress.
— Einhard
Charlemagne is rather handsome. He has a large nose, a 
short neck, a high-thin voice, a mustache, but no beard: 
Charlemagne was strong and well built. He was tall in 
stature, but not excessively so, for his height was just seven 
times the length of his own feet. His health was good. (…) 
His eyes were piercing and unusually large. His expres -
sion was gay and good-humored. Whether he was seated 
or standing, he always appeared masterful and dignified. 
— Einhard
He usually wears fairly common clothes with a golden 
brooch and a very fine belt. Regalia is reserved for when foreign 
ambassadors are present. Only after 800 does Charlemagne wear 
a beard and an imperial eagle on his helmet.

Archbishop Turpin (734–778)
T urpin was a monk at Saint Denis who was appointed 
Archbishop of Reims, but decided to take up arms on the death of his 
father. He soon became the glorious warrior-archbishop of the Franks. 
By his double task as a knight and a bishop, he embodies the Church’s 
support of Charlemagne’s holy wars against the pagans. T urpin is one 
of Charlemagne’s closest friends, as well as the royal biographer.

Ogier the Dane (740–814)
King Godfrid of Denmark’s semi-giant son was raised at the 
castle of Boulogne as King Pepin’s war hostage. There he married 
the castle warden’s daughter, Bellisende, who gave him two sons: 
Baldwin and Dudo (both half-giants). For a while, Duke Naymo’s 
nephew lived as a respected senior squire at King Pepin’s court.
The fearless Ogier always remains loyal to the paladins, yet 
at the same time is often in conflict with King Charlemagne. 
The irate and stubborn Dane brings his king victories in battle 
and defeats cumbersome giants, as well as occasionally fighting 
the Franks and their king. After a long period of captivity, Ogier 
goes to the East and has some exciting adventures on his own, 
before returning to the Frankish court once more.

Roland (750–778)
Roland was born when his parents, Princess Bertha and Count 
Milo of Aiglent, were living in exile. Once accepted back at court, 
Roland becomes Charlemagne’s favorite nephew. The king pro-
motes him Count of the Breton March in 768. He is the companion 
of Oliver, and becomes engaged to Oliver’s sister, Aude, in 771. He is 
generally recognized as the world’s best and most chivalrous knight. 
When Roland acquires Durendal and Hector’s Armor during the 
first Spanish Campaign, he becomes nigh-invincible.

Astolf of England (751–814)
King Offa sends his second son to the court of his Frankish 
ally in 773. Though Astolf is not particularly skilled in combat, 
he is known for his good looks, courage, wisdom and his chival -
rous courtesy: “ a courtly, gallant gentleman,/courteous, no -
ble, fair and free.” Orlando Innamorato , 1.7.LXIII
Even before he becomes a paladin in 779 after the battle 
of Roncevaux, he has acquired some very valuable magic items, 
like the Golden Lance, the Horn of T error, and a Book of Spell 
Breaking. His most notable feat of arms is the capture of the 
Saracen robber giant Caligorante (775), and, riding a hippogriff, 
he travels to Africa and the moon to recover Roland’s lost wits 
(776). Later in life, he retires as a hermit. At the very end of his 
life, Sir Astolf becomes a saint-bishop in Mayence (813).

William Shortnose (756–812)
William’s father, Aymeri, is the conqueror of Narbonne 
and the heir of Garin of Monglane. William is the bravest 
among his six brothers and, like them, he does not inherit a fief 
from his father. He remains a poor bachelor knight until he is 
given the title of Duke of Septimania at the age of 34 (in 790). 
He marries the Moorish princess Orable (Emir Tiebaut’s wife), 
baptized Guibourc.
William is the leader of the reconquest of Provence, in 
which he takes an active part by liberating the cities of Nîmes 
and Orange. He is the symbol of loyalty to the Frankish 
crown, and serves both Charlemagne and King Louis with 
unrelenting zeal.
William is optimistic, and has a recognizable boisterous 
laugh. When angered, though, he quickly loses his temper. On 
several occasions, when he is refused access to a castle or city, he 
kills the porter, but afterwards he always pays their wergild.

Paladins
T
he list of paladins given below is not necessarily com -
plete. Especially from the 780’s onward, not all of the 12 
places are filled, which leaves a good occasion for Player-
knights to earn the title of paladin for themselves.
Anseïs of Carthago: The son of Ripay of Brittany, who died 
heroically at Roncevaux, marries the Moorish princess Gaudissa 
of Cordoba and is made Duke of Spain in 779. However brave 
and well-intentioned, young Anseïs is unable to resist an alliance 
of Frankish traitors and vengeful Moors. At his death, his daugh-
ter Galienne (from a previous marriage) takes Count Oliver of 
Bourges as her husband.
Anseïs the Proud: Proud Anseïs is the son of the Viscount 
of Dijon, and one of the first paladins, knighted on the battle -
field together with Roland, Ogier, Oliver and the others. 
Aymonnet: Renaud’s eldest son becomes a knight in 790 
and a paladin in 795. His main feats of arms are directed against 
the hated Mayence clan.
Baldwin the Brave: Ganelon’s third son, whom he had 
with his second wife, Princess Bertha, is a courteous and roman-
tic knight, who is naturally accepted as a paladin in 779. He is 
Roland’s half-brother and the companion of Berard of Ardennes, 
who joins the order two years later. Baldwin rapidly distinguish-
es himself in the wars against the Saxons, and the young paladin 
even briefly becomes the first Frankish Duke of Saxony in 782, 
after he marries Widukind’s converted wife, Sebile. T ragically, he 
dies at the siege of T remoigne a year after.
Berard of Ardennes: When the eldest son of Thierry of 
Ardennes comes of age, Charlemagne dubs him knight and pal-
adin, and he becomes the companion of his friend Baldwin the 
Brave. Like his friend, the reckless and lusty Berard dies in the 
war with the Saxons.
Berenger the Gascon: This swift young man distinguishes 
himself at the Battle of the Humble Squires, where he is knight-
ed and made a paladin by Charlemagne. Odo’s companion likes 
to call himself the best horseman of Frankland.
Bertrand of Narbonne: William’s nephew accompanies 
his famous uncle in his adventures in Provence. He attains pal -
adinhood at an early age, in 792, like his cousin and companion

Eric of Friuli: The elder brother of Queen Hildegard be -

Eric is a born leader and a fine tactician, but dies in battle against 
the rebellious Avars. His marshal, the cynical demi-giant Eishere, 
is one of the best sword fighters of Frankland.
Galien the Restored: The illegitimate son of Sir Oliver 
and the Byzantine princess Iacobina was raised in Byzantium. 
He travels to Frankland in 788 and recovers his father’s fief 
(hence his nickname). After his promise to avenge his father’s 
death on the Moors, Charlemagne makes Galien a paladin. Later 
he returns to Byzantium and finds his mother has been captured 
by the Persians. He liberates his mother and becomes King of 
Jerusalem in 802, after Sultan Norandin’s defeat by Count 
Simon of Apulia.
Geoffrey of Anjou: At Thierry’s death in Saxony in 786, 
his young son Geoffrey continues his late father’s mission of 
fighting the pagans, whether in Saxony, Slavonia, or Spain. 
Geoffrey is a handsome, gentle knight, who turns into a real sav-
age on the battlefield.
Gerard of Anjou: T ogether with his companion, Engelier, 
Gerard is dubbed as a paladin in 773. He is a very religious man, 
whose spartan and frugal lifestyle almost turns him into a saint.
Gerold II of Bavaria: When the king marries Queen 
Hildegard in 771, Gerold, her brother, becomes not only the 
king’s brother-in-law, but also the royal sword master. Later, 
when Charlemagne disposes of the disloyal T assilo III, he makes 
Gerold Duke of Bavaria and a paladin of his court (789) as the 
companion of Eric of Friuli. A competent army leader, Gerold 
dies in battle against the Avars. He is remembered for his un -
equalled sword skill and his outstanding valor.
Guy of Burgundy: Handsome Guy is a nephew of Y on 
of Gascony, and the illustrious Garin of Monglane is his great-
grand-uncle. Guy is both a skilled battle commander and a ro -
mantic soul who is always in for a laugh.
Hamon of Galicia: The courteous Duke of Galicia be -
comes a paladin at Charlemagne’s court as Sir Raimbold’s com-
panion. He dies in battle against the Avars.

Bordelais: Duke Sevin I of Bordeaux is the leader of this

Etichonids: A family of strong but honest brutes from

Gerardines: Gerard of Paris has two sons: Bego, who suc -

Septimania, and Lothair, who becomes a count in Aquitaine. 
The Gerardines can trace their genealogy back to Charles

House of Lorraine: An ancient and respected house of 
Austrasia, where they control many monasteries. Although they 
were victorious in their bloody feud against the Bordelais not 
very long ago, this has, paradoxically, weakened them. Many 
young Lorrainer widows remarried, which broke up the family 
coherence. 
Lombards: Rivals of the Carolingians. They understand -
ably develop a grudge against Charlemagne when he shames 
Desideria by repudiating her, then conquers Lombardy and 
sends Desiderius to a monastery, wages war against Arichis of 
Benevento, and deposes T assilo III of Bavaria. Members of the 
Lombard royal line are Desiderius, his son Adalgis, and his four 
daughters Liutperga, Desideria, Gerberga and Adalperga, re -
spectively married to T assilo III, Charlemagne, Carloman II and 
Arichis of Benevento.
Mayence: The Austrasian Mayence clan is indirectly re -
lated to the Arnulfings by Princess Alpaïda the Saxon. The de -
scendants of Grifo of Hautefeuille, fourth son of the illustrious 
Doon of Mayence, are seen as clearly separated from the other 
houses founded by old Doon’s many sons. The Mayence Clan 
is the second most influential house of the kingdom; respected 
and feared by many, and hated by some. Almost all vile traitors 
in the stories are from the wicked Mayence Clan, such as Sir 
Ganelon and Sir Hardrad.
Monglane or Narbonnais: Although far from wealthy, the 
Narbonnais are the single most powerful house in Septimania, 
and later Provence. The branch to which Sir Oliver belongs con-
trols much of Burgundy. The Narbonnais supply many famous 
heroes and even paladins including William Shortnose and his 
nephew, Bertrand.
Nibelungs: Charlemagne’s wealthy and loyal allies and 
relatives descend from Charles Martel’s brother Childebrand I. 
They live in Neustria, and particularly Normandy. Most of them 
are thoughtful and scrupulous men.
Rorgonids: A proud family from Normandy, led by Duke 
Richard of Normandy and later by Count Rorgo of Maine, the 
lover of Princess Rothrud.
Widonids: Another great family allied to the Arnulfings 
have their fiefs in the Mosel region, around Reims and T rier, 
the Breton March, and Italy. The best known leader of this line 
is the merciless Wido, who succeeds Roland as Count of the 
Breton March in 778. His brother W arnar serves King Louis of 
Aquitaine.
Magicians
Angelica: A stunning and chaste princess of Cathay, who 
wears a powerful magic ring of invisibility and magic negation. 
She may herself produce only minor enchantments, but her su -
pernatural beauty (APP 30) often works as a Love spell on men. 
She is saved from a sea monster by Rogero (775) and wooed by 
Roland, but finally falls in love with a young Moor, Medoro,

Atlantes: The most powerful Saracen astrologer and sor -
cerer lives in a magic mountain castle. He loves Rogero as his 
son, and tries to protect him from harm and adventure by lock -
ing him up inside his steel prison-palace. The deceitful and cun-
ning Atlantes owns a powerful spell book of illusions, a Shield 
of Blinding and a hippogriff. Carelessness is his weakness. The 
magician finally dies of a broken heart when Rogero leaves, con-
verts to Christianity, and marries Bradamant.
Basin (Elegast): The Frankish Count of Geneva is a skillful 
enchanter, who was born and raised at his fief of Huy. He mainly 
uses herbs to produce magic effects such as opening locks, un -
derstanding animal speech, putting people to sleep, and healing 
wounds. Ever loyal to Charlemagne, he nevertheless is accused 
of betrayal and destituted (781). Impoverished, he becomes the 
robber knight Elegast, wandering through the Austrasian for -
ests. Charlemagne forgives Basin and reinstates him in 786. At 
the end of his life he becomes the Archbishop of T rier (802). He 
has three sons: Berenger, Renier, and Aubery.
Maugis: The most famous Frankish wizard-knight, from

his magic when all other means have failed. He uses powders to

Enemies Within
No king is free of internal intrigue, and Charlemagne is 
no exception. 
The Aquitainians
The followers of Duke Hunold of Aquitaine, who is killed 
in 769, claim that at least one of the late duke’s children is still 
alive and can pretend to the lordship of Aquitaine. They do not 
foment open rebellion, but they use rather subversive ways of 
achieving their objectives.
It is noteworthy that the Frankish king almost never visits 
the southern part of the Franklands. T o consolidate his power 
in the south, Charlemagne has his son Louis crowned King of 
Aquitaine in 781 with the help of William Shortnose. On this 
occasion, nobles such as Arneïs and the relatives of old Richard 
of Orleans cause quite a stir.
Afterwards, the Aquitainian faction at court is seriously 
weakened, but their thirst for independence and their personal 
ambitions of power are still very much alive. 
The Bavarian-Lombard Alliance
At his brother’s unfortunate death in 771, Charlemagne 
claims the lands of Carloman II and becomes the sole leader of 
the Franks. This shocks several high noblemen, since normally 
Carloman’s title should have been passed on to his two healthy 
sons, for whom Charlemagne refuses to appoint a regent. Rather 
than serve a usurper, Ogier and his friends openly rebel against 
their new king. When Carloman’s widow, Gerberga of Pavia, 
flees to her father’s court, Frankish nobles accompany the prin-
cess and her two sons to Lombardy.
Charlemagne captures his sister-in-law and his two young 
cousins, as well as Ogier the Dane, during his successful cam -
paign against the Lombards in 773. Charlemagne sends the 
royal Lombards (King Desiderius, Queen Ansa, Princess 
Gerberga and her sons) to separate monasteries, and he has 
Ogier locked up in a castle under Duke Naymo’s care. As far 
as the king is concerned, the Lombard royal dynasty is no 
more, and Charlemagne distributes titles and lands to his loyal 
Frankish followers.
Most surviving Lombard nobles swear fealty to their new 
king. But not all of them. Others prefer to flee, especially to 
Avarland or Bavaria. They are welcomed with open arms at the 
Agilolfing court, as Duke T assilo of Bavaria is himself married 
to the Lombard Princess Liutperga. Indeed, the disinherited 
Gerberga and her relatives have many powerful discontented 
friends. Among their allies are some Franks who remain loyal to 
Carloman II and his sons. Abroad, the Bavarian-Lombard fac -
tion is supported by Constantinople (the refuge of the vengeful 
Prince Adalgise), the Avars, and the independent Italian duchies 
of Benevento, Spoleto and Friuli.
The Clan of Mayence
The House of Mayence is one of the most powerful clans 
of the kingdom. Their leader, Ganelon of Ponthieu, Count of 
Mainz, is a trusted councilor until he betrays his lord king out 
of hate for Roland, his step-son. Ganelon is accused after the 
battle of Roncevaux. His honor is defended by his champion 
and cousin, Pinabel the Gascon (an excellent swordsman and a 
skilled thief ), who loses his judicial duel against Count Thierry 
of Anjou. Ganelon is quartered by horses.
Apart from Baldwin the Brave, all other Mayence knights 
are black-hearted traitors and ideally suited to figure as villains 
in an adventure. They want power by all means and, not surpris-
ingly, they are among the founders of the Black Knights.
The Revolting Barons
Regularly, wronged vassals — including some paladins — 
oppose King Charlemagne. Even though they continue to rec -
ognize him as their lawful king, and respect him as such, they 
fight him to obtain justice or reparation for a crime. The four 
sons of Count Aymon defy the king’s power for several years, 
just like their cousin Maugis and their friend Ogier the Dane. 
Other powerful counts, like Gerard of Vienne and his family, or 
Garnier and Guy of Nanteuil, refuse to submit to Charlemagne 
on his conditions. All these revolting barons end up making 
peace, after having defeated their king in battle, or a forceful in-
tervention from the paladins, or a miracle from God.
Pepin the Hunchback
Charlemagne’s repudiated son, sweet-faced but unfortu -
nately hunchbacked, lives a miserable life in the court’s kitch -
ens. His mother Himiltrude is safely locked away in a nunnery. 
But at court, flatterers tell Pepin that he should take his life 
back into his own hands, change his destiny and become king, 
or even emperor, himself ! These “friends” would gladly help 
to install Pepin on the Frankish throne in order to obtain high 
positions for themselves. On several occasions, high nobles 
conspire against their king in order to seize the throne. Count 
Hardrad of Thuringia comes close in 785, but his vast conspir -
acy ultimately fails.

The Black Knights
After the failed coup of Count Hardrad, some of his rel -
atives ally themselves with the House of Mayence. They create 
a secret but influential brotherhood, the Black Knights, whose 
first leader is Macharias of Lausanne, Hardrad’s nephew. Their 
goal is to further their political interests by manipulating Prince 
Charlot, Charlemagne’s favorite son. The Black Knights are 
mostly courtiers, always intriguing and scheming.
Whenever their leader sends them out to do dirty business 
(up to assassination), the anonymous knights all dress in black, 
hence the name of their organization. They recognize each other 
by a small secret token, like a blue stone or a ribbon. The prince 
himself almost never participates and usually stays back at court. 
However, in 809 the Black Knights and their leader, Amaury de 
Hauteville, ambush Huon of Bordeaux and his men, and in the 
ensuing fight Prince Charlot is killed.
Amaury of Hauteville: The last member of the House of 
Mayence becomes Count of Mayence in 803. A few years lat -
er, he replaces Fulco of Morillon as the chief of Charlot’s Black 
Knights. Amaury is a dastardly backstabber, corrupt and cruel, 
fatally proud, and over-confident.

is even more cruel and hateful than his father, hence his nick -
name. During his long and violent reign (780–808), he gets 
briefly imprisoned by his rival Deramay (793–794), but returns 
to power and systematically eliminates all those he suspects of 
disloyalty, causing long-lasting revolts and unrest all over the 
emirate.
Deramay the Usurper: The deceitful and vain W ali of 
Cordoba captures Emir Marsile II and claims the title of emir 
for himself (793–794).

Isabella of Galicia, for whom he builds a bridge and a tomb. In 
her memory, he defends the bridge leading to the funerary mon-
ument for an entire year. He is killed by Roland at Bradamant’s 
wedding (777).

Widukind: His name means “child of the forest.” Widukind 
is the uncontested leader of the W estphalians, and later even of

Widukind is allied with the Danes, with whom he relent -
lessly fights the hated Franks, until he is finally captured and
