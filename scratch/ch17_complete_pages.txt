The sections on foreign culture are necessarily unfair. 
History is written by victors, and the descriptions below con -
vey a strong Frankish bias. For example, the epics transform the 
splendid culture of Al-Andalus into the rather unsophisticated 
polytheistic pagan Moors.
While some cultural evolution occurs (especially the 
adoption of Frankish customs, such as Knighthood, Chivalry 
and Romance), foreign armor and weapons do not evolve over 
time, as the Franks’ do. Many foreigners visit the Franklands to 
learn the virtues of knighthood, and they may have purchased 
or won current Frankish equipment, but the vast majority of 
the warriors in a foreign army are armed the same at the end of 
Charlemagne’s reign as at the beginning.

Appearance
These stocky mountain folks are not very tall (–1 SIZ), 
but usually strongly-built, resilient (+1 CON) and dark-haired. 
Some have blue eyes, but all have a proud, penetrating look that 
puts foreigners ill at ease.
Character
The main Basques characteristic is their deep sense of inde-
pendence and pride (Proud). They are very intolerant of authority 
(Arbitrary) and are often described as taciturn, treacherous and fickle 
(Deceitful). They obey only to their family and clan (Love [family]).
Skills
The Basques are widely known for their keen sight and 
hearing (Awareness), and for their numerous legends and super-
stitions (Folk Lore, Faerie Lore).
Relations with the Franks
The Basque country is part of the duchy of Gascony and, 
as such, a part of the Frankish kingdom. However, when Duke 
Lupus of Gascony does homage to Charlemagne in 768, the 
Basques revolt and become a semi-independent nation whose 
leader pays only lip-service to the Franks.
In 778, some local chieftains ally with the Moors at the 
slaughter of Roncevaux. At the death of their leader they once 
more pay homage to the Franks, but Charlemagne’s authority 
over the Basques remains weak at best.

Territory
The relatively small Basque country is located in the west -
ern Pyrenees, with the River Ebro at its southern border. The 
western Pyrenees are inhospitable and mountainous, and can 
only be crossed in summer via the Roncevaux Pass. Even then, 
foreign travelers need a local guide.
The mild sea climate provides enough rain water to irrigate 
the fertile green hills and mountain slopes. In the mountains, 
sudden fog and mist sometimes cover entire valleys.
Places of Interest
Pa MPlo Na  
This walled city is the only urban center in Basque Country. 
Pamplona is the seat of the duke, who lives in a wooden castle on 
the River Ebro.
r o NcevauX  Pass
This dangerous narrow pass on the border with Gascony is 
the only path by which to cross the western Pyrenees into Spain.
Society
Each Basque clan is ruled by a patriarch as far as military 
matters go. In case of conflict, he commands the warriors and 
leads all negotiations, consulting his kinsmen when necessary. 
The Basques have no ambition of conquering or subjugating 
their neighbors, but neither will they ever surrender to any for -
eign people.
Religion
The Basques have been converted to Christianity only re -
cently. Almost no ecclesiastic structure exists yet, as they have 
but a single bishop (in Pamplona), very few priests, and no mon-
asteries at all.
Not surprisingly, many pagan habits persist, such as gath -
ering in mountain caves for goat sacrifices. Pagan Basques 
worship the weather gods Sugaar and Mari, who are supposed 
to live on mountain peaks. Hairy savages and gentle giants 
are said to inhabit the highest mountain regions as well, espe -
cially where standing stones can be found. Another fabulous 
creature is the lamiak , a bird-footed nymph living near lakes 
or springs.
Daily Life and Economy
Pamplona, the seat of the duke, is the only Basque town. 
Basques live almost exclusively in poor mountain villages, where 
they practice sheep herding. Their way of life is completely 
self-sufficient, so they trade very little with the outside world.
W omen have a large role in Basque society. Indeed, fami -
lies are based on matrilineal kinship. W omen have a voice as es-
teemed councilors and they may own property and land.
Warfare
Basques fight as mountain warriors. They have no cavalry, 
though they use their sturdy mountain ponies to carry equip -
ment. They try to avoid open battle in the plains, preferring hit-
and-run guerrilla warfare and ambushes, which allow them to 
take full advantage of the mountainous terrain.
Standard Equipment
Nobles: Leather armor (6 points), wooden shield (6 
points); spear, sword, dagger.
Footmen: Animal skin armor (4 points), small wooden 
shield (4 points); spear or javelins, hand axe, bow or sling, dagger.
Code of Honor
Knighthood, and chivalry especially, are unknown to the 
Basques. Their individual honor is based solely on family repu -
tation. As a result, family feuds are numerous and long-lasting. 
The Basques are fundamentally hostile to any centralized power 
and are only superficially feudalized, having preserved many el -
ements of their tribal society. Therefore, they often refuse to do 
homage or swear fealty to a Frankish overlord. When they do, 
such an oath has no value for them and they unhesitatingly break 
any promise that goes against the interest of their clan or family. 
Fortifications
The Basques do not build castles. In case of impending 
trouble, wooden watch towers are used to inform the popula -
tion, who may flee into hidden mountain caves if necessary.

The Bretons are uneducated pagans, despite efforts to

Daily Life and Economy
The people usually live in a small village on top of a hill, 
protected by a wooden palisade. Most houses are square wooden 
dwellings with low thatched roofs. The locals make a living from 
fishing and cattle herding. The whole of Brittany is quite poor, 
and trade with other lands almost non-existent.

Territory and Nations
The term “Britons” is a generic name for all of the peoples 
inhabiting the British Isles, including the regions of Cornwall, 
Ireland, Mercia, Northumbria, Scotland, W ales and W essex. 
The Isles are mostly covered by fertile green valleys and roll-

Scotland are not very high. The climate is often cheerless, foggy, 
and wet.
The island’s ancient Roman roads have fallen somewhat in 
disrepair, but are still used by merchants and other travelers, in 
addition to the navigable rivers and streams. The major trading 
ports by which foreigners reach the Isles are Dover and London.

Society
As in Frankland, noblemen own the land and the feudal 
system produces knights and lords who rule over the peasant 
population. At the highest level, the British lands form a confed-
eration, a so-called Heptarchy, where all seven territories remain 
independent but are allied in case of a foreign invasion. Each 
land is ruled by a clan chieftain, or king. The most powerful clan 
leader, usually the King of Mercia, is considered high-king of the 
Britons. At times, two clan leaders fight over the crown and it 
even happens that two rival kings exist at the same time, causing 
bloody internal strife.
Religion
The inhabitants of Britannia are faithful Christians. Many 
churches and cathedrals are being built, and the British monas -
teries are renowned for their austere spiritual training, especially 
in the relatively isolated regions of Northumbria, Ireland and 
Scotland. In these religious establishments monks and copyist 
are preserving rare books, and exchanges with Frankish abbeys 
are frequent.
Daily Life and Economy
Apart from a few urban centers like London or Y ork, most 
Britons live on the countryside in small farming communities. 
The peasants make their living from herding, especially sheep 
and goats. Along the coast, fishermen sail out daily on their small 
boats. In general, the Britons are not as rich as the Franks.
Warfare
British lords are often not as well equipped as their Frankish 
counterparts. They largely rely on levies of scantily armored 
footmen, with only a few knights to command them. The rare 
and expensive horses are saved for riding and pursuit, rarely rid-
den in combat. In battle they make full use of archery.
There is one point at which the Britons are superior to 
Charlemagne: their fleet is better equipped and organized, 
though still unable to rival the sea-borne Danes.

Territory
Although the Eastern Roman Empire (now called the 
Byzantine Empire or simply Byzantium) is declining, it still 
covers a vast geographical area. T o the east lies the thinly pop -
ulated Anatolian mountain region, which has hot, dry summers 
and cold, wet winters. It is mainly used to pasture livestock. The 
rest of the empire (including Constantinople) consists of coastal 
plains with a mild climate, suitable for raising grain, vegetables, 
vines, and olives. 
Many different people live in all these regions, but they 
are all Byzantines. The mainland is divided into 6 themata (the 
equivalent of duchies). Apart from Constantinople, the most 
important cities are T essalonica, Adrianople, Nicaea, Smyrna, 
Ephesus, Ancyra, Amorium, Attalia and T rebizond.
The Byzantines hold power over many isles including Crete 
and Cyprus, and they have managed to cling to their Italian ter-
ritories: Ravenna (seat of the exarch, the representative of the 
Byzantine Emperor in Italy), V enice and Istria in the north, the 
duchy of Calabria and Sicily in the south. These enclaves behave 
as if they are allies, rather than Byzantine possessions.
T o go to Byzantium via the Danube is virtually impossible 
because of the hostile Avars. T raveling by boat from Italy is the 
safest and easiest route.
Places of Interest
Constantinople: The famous and ancient capital of 
Byzantium is surrounded by impressive city walls and guard -
ed by a thousand knights. Its fresh water is supplied by a great 
aqueduct. The marvelous palace of the Byzantine Emperor, 
called the Hieria, rotates around a huge central pillar and is 
ornamented with moving and singing statues. The inhabitants 
of Constantinople are very fond of chariot races which are or -
ganized at the Hippodrome on special occasions. The city is 
the empire’s political, economic and cultural center, and it’s 
by far the largest and wealthiest city in Europe. The churches 
have golden roofs! Still, crime is almost unknown since the city 
guards maintain a very high level of vigilance. The capital har -
bors a small minority of Jewish merchants.

by ministers ( logothetes), great nobles ( patricians), and military

Warfare
The Byzantines are constantly at war with the Huns 
from Bulgaria, the Persians (to whom they sometimes have to 
pay heavy tributes in return for peace), and the Saracens from 
Babylon. On the other hand, the Khazars (Circassians) are nat -
ural allies against the Persians. Without declaring open war, 
Empress Irene starts to colonize deserted Slavic lands on the 
European mainland.
Armies and Tactics
The Byzantine troops are a professional army, whose sala -
ries are paid by the emperor. Armor and weapons are manufac -
tured at state factories. They wear Roman-style uniforms and 
include many foreign mercenaries. They are well equipped, but 
have low morale and their war leaders often serve different in -
terests. Moreover, the Byzantines often hire additional foreign 
mercenaries in the wars against the Bulgars or Slavs. Their army 
standard is the double-headed eagle.
There are several army ranks, comparable to the Frankish 
ones: cataphract (knight), theme (vassal), tagmata (scara), droun-
garios (banneret), turmarch (count), strategos (duke). Imperial 
tagmata are heavy cavalry units under the direct command of 
the basileus. They operate as body guards and are quartered at 
the palace in Constantinople. 
The Byzantines have a large fleet, which gets almost entirely 
destroyed in a civil war in 802–803. In sieges or naval battles, the 
Byzantines make use of their feared and almost-inextinguishable 
Greek fire.

Terrain Types

Society
A single king rules over a society which is divided into class-
es: nobles are called jarls, landowning freemen are carls, land-
less serfs are thralls, and at the bottom of the social scale we find 
slaves, usually captured during raids in Slavonia, who wear iron 
bands around their necks.
Groups of families together form clans, led by its most glo -
rious member, usually a rich older jarl. Each clan holds annual 
assemblies: the landthings. These are popular meetings where 
free men unite under the leadership of powerful landholders. 
A law court is held, usually accompanied by a great market. On 
the highest level, the clan leaders meet at the allthing, under the 
supervision of the king himself. Legal disputes may be settled by 
ordeals, judicial duels or by paying wergild.
Religion

the open fields. The dead are either cremated or buried in grave 
mounds along with valuable possessions.
Skalds are the Danish poets and singers. They recite the 
laws of the land and sometimes act as independent itinerant 
judges. They sing of heroic deeds and the exotic creatures known 
to inhabit the North, such as mermaids, dragons, underground 
dwarfs and goblins.

Warfare
At the end of the eighth century the coastlines of England 
and Frankland come under attack from Danish raiders. They 
even threaten the prosperous port of Dorestad. Their favorite

Society
The Lombard king resides permanently in his magnificent 
court at Pavia. He is allied with the Bavarians and Byzantium.
Lombard cities are governed by urban counts named gast-

entirely without defense.

loyal followers or appease rivals. So, even though the concepts of 
knighthood and chivalry (and later even Romance as well) have 
found their way to Saracen society, there is no tightly-structured 
feudal hierarchy.
Saracen culture is deeply suffused with ancient tribal re -
lations, where empty titles and the prestige of lineage is more 
important than land ownership and military prowess. The 
delicate power balance between the great families depends on 
temporary alliances. T ribes or military confederations live in 
geographical isolation from one another and sometimes have 
opposing interests. Internecine wars and rebellions are frequent, 
so the emir must rule with both delicacy and firmness.
In reality, money is what really holds Saracen society to -
gether. Gold and silk are all that count for the rich middle class 
of artisans and merchants. Their non-feudal commercial inter -
ests often effectively harness the wali’s tribal interests and his 
military ambitions.
Justice is concentrated in the towns. Indeed, as a ruling mi-
nority in Spain, the Moors have established their power base in-
side fortified cities, leaving the direct control of the countryside 
to local rulers, often Mozarabs. These rural lords have a certain 
autonomy, as long as they pay tribute to the wali. In all Saracen 
towns, the wali has the right to demand the inhabitants to spend 
a specified time working on road or bridge maintenance. 
The primary Saracen weakness is their internal division, 
even to the point where the emir calls on Christians or Jews for 
delicate and confidential tasks. Such persons are often promoted 
to high administrative offices. The death of an emir or sultan in-
variably gives rise to internal strife and a ferocious power struggle 
between influential families.
In Spain the authority of the emir is unstable, for many 
walis act as more or less independent rulers, and these urban 
magnates themselves are confronted with rural lords claiming 
autonomy. Feudalism is only loosely established and the emir 
wields little power outside the main cities. The walis are often 
tempted to assert their independence from Cordoba and look to 
the Franks for support, though the last thing they want is to live 
under Frankish dominion.
Since the wars between the Moors and Visigoths in the first 
half of the 8th century, the frontier region between the emirate 
and the Visigothic kingdom of Asturias has been almost devoid 
of human habitation. Empty frontier zones, known as thagr, sep-
arate the Moors from their northern neighbors (Visigoths and 
Basques) and are defended by a line of advanced watch towers 
and small castles. Boars, bears and wolves roam unmolested in 
these uninhabited, often sterile, buffer territories.

The Mozarabs are the Arabic-speaking, Visigothic Christian 
population of Moorish Spain. T o practice their religion, Christians 
and Jews must pay the dhimmi, an annual poll-tax. This protects 
them from persecution and even gives them certain rights, but 
excludes them from holding political power inside the cities. 
Christians and Jews have their own churches and courts.
The Christian churches on the Spanish peninsula hold 
many saints’ and martyrs’ relics, which attract emissaries from 
Frankish bishops and abbots who wish to buy (or steal) them.
Daily Life and Economy
A small number of Saracens are nomadic desert folk who ride 
on camels and live in tents. However, most Saracens live in cities. 
Houses are usually built from tamped earth. Since water is a con-
stant problem, all cities are situated in river valleys. The surround-
ing fields are irrigated either by chain wells (a series of linked un-
derground wells), or by norias (waterwheels with buckets driven 
by donkeys in order to lift underground water to the field).
The numerous, often very large, cities have very narrow and sin-
uous streets, and in their central market squares a temple can usually 
be found. A Master of the Market (sahib al-suq) controls this central 
area, which is strictly non-residential and deserted at night, when it is 
patrolled by guards. Jews usually have their own walled quarter. The 
wali lives in his citadel castle (alcazar) along the city wall.
Saracen society has given rise to burgeoning urban-craft in-
dustries. Even during times of war, many caravans travel along 
the main trade routes, though political instability does not fa -
vor the commercial routes from Frankland leading into Spain 
via Saragossa. Saracen merchants trade with all other cultures 
around the Mediterranean Sea. Indeed, trade is so important 
in the Saracen world that their entire empire is a free trade area 
where one’s origin is no bar to travel. Foreign merchants are 
treated with respect, for commercial interests outweigh military 
hostility. Like the Byzantines and Persians, Saracen merchants 
prefer gold coins or silk bundles to silver pieces.
Saracens are especially known for their flourishing slave 
trade. Note that no Moorish or Saracen slaves are allowed, nor 
can law-abiding tax-paying foreigners be enslaved. Slaves in 
Spain tend to be employed as domestic servants, administrators 
and soldiers, rather than peasants obliged to work in the fields.
Moorish cities are famous for their leather, camels and pure-
bred horses. Though well supplied with most luxury goods (silks, 
perfume, glass), the Moors have a chronic need for wood, since 
their lands are not very forested. The rural population, mostly 
Visigothic, make their living from sheep herding and farming ce-
real crops, grapevines and olive trees. Like the Persians, the Moors 
and Saracens practice intensive hydraulic agriculture around their 
cities. Inside the towns and cities lofty gardens produce fruits.
Warfare
The Saracens have a permanent standing army composed 
of askaris (warriors) and foreign mercenary soldiers. The better 
equipped nobles are faris, the equivalent of the Frankish knights. 
An almansour is a sort of banneret, while a wali is the ruler over a 
town or a small district (a kura, plural kuwar), quite like a count. 
A king is called an emir. The highest rank is that of sultan, com-
parable to an emperor. Saracens sometimes count enslaved giants 
or centaurs among their ranks. The Moors carry a white stan -
dard, the Sultan of Babylon raises a green banner.
While they sometimes engage in full scale battles with the 
Franks, the Saracens are more feared for their quick raids and 
their piracy on the Mediterranean Sea. Their goal is often not 
conquest but the capture of plunder and slaves.

Territory and Nations
Persia: The lands of Persia lie in the fertile river valleys be-
yond the Arabian desert, bordered in the east by the sheer, im -
passable Zagros Mountains.
Holy Lands: The land around the Holy City from the 
Mediterranean coast to the Jordan River is called the Holy Land 
by the Christians.
Terrain Types
The coastal areas and the cultivated valleys of the Tigris and 
the Euphrates are irrigated by an intricate system of canals. The 
rest of the Persian lands consist of sandy or stony deserts, some 
grassy plains and dry rocky hills and mountains.
Climate
Persia has very hot, dry summers and rather cold winters. 
Sand storms occasionally occur during the hot season.
Travel
Most voyagers take a boat following one of the great rivers. 
Land travel through the desert is done on camels, by ponies or 
mules in hilly or grassy terrain.
Places of Interest
Baghdad: The fabled home of the caliph is situated on the 
banks of the Tigris River. It is the commercial, scientific and political 
heart of Persia, and one of the biggest and richest cities in the world. 
In its center lies a peninsula surrounded by triple walls, named the 
Round City. It holds the luxuriously-decorated Great T emple, the 
caliph’s Golden Gate Palace with its emerald dome, the delicious 
Palace Gardens, and the residential quarter for the guards, senior 
officers and high noblemen. The Round City’s four gates are locked 
at night. Canals crisscross through Baghdad’s surrounding popular 
quarters, which are inhabited by merchants and artisans.
Damascus: The City of Jasmine lies on the Great Road 
leading from the Mediterranean Sea to Baghdad. It is defended 
by a square citadel and a high city wall with seven gates. The 
Grand T emple, holding several Christian relics, and the T reasury 
T ower can be found on the central town square. The population 
is a mix of Christians, Jews, Persians and other peoples, such as 
Byzantine and Saracen traders. Once the capital of the caliphate, 
it is now the home of some Persian dissidents and rebels.

Society
The Persian empire is vast and immensely rich. It is gov -
erned by a caliph, who is assisted in all daily business by a vizier 
and a court magician. In the cities, a qadi (judge) dispenses jus -
tice in the caliph’s name, and street militias maintain law and 
order. Local noblemen hold the surrounding lands and access to 
sources of water. In return, they send the caliph knights and sol-
diers in times of war.
This enlightened people accepts and respects Christians and 
Jews, but they come to fear and hate the neighboring Saracens, with 
whom they are allied until the disastrous campaign against Rome in 
767. The Holy Land receives substantial financial support from the 
Persians, the Byzantines, and Charlemagne, who sends money for 
the construction of churches, hostelries and a library.
Religion
The Persian pagans worship the Sun. They pray together 
with their slaves, but they do not use books nor do they have 
priests. Some of them dance and chant in an enthralling fashion 
while praying.
Daily Life and Economy
Most Persians live close to a source of water, which they use 
to irrigate their fields. Other inhabited areas are the oases in the 
desert or the serails along the main caravan trails, where travelers 
and merchants spend time to rest and replenish their food and 
water supplies. Note that Persian food is very odd to Frankish 
taste and stomachs.
The great Persian cities are supported by intensive irrigation 
agriculture. The trade in gold, horses, silk, spices and slaves is 
the prime source of wealth. Persian merchants have commercial 
relations with almost all known countries, including far Cathay 
and Sericane. Thanks to their far-flung, international contacts, 
the Persians have made significant scientific progress in many

Territory
The ground is covered with glacial debris, sand, and peat 
bogs. The landscape is dominated by rivers and forests. The 
Saxon homeland is mostly covered with impenetrable hilly for -
ests and swamps. In fact, the only areas not naturally forested are 
the high mountains, bogs, and coastal marshes.
Frisia is the western coastal area, characterized by wide, 
poorly-drained salt marshes covered with peat, cut by meander-
ing creeks and tidal inlets, bays and river estuaries. Off the coast 
lie many sand banks and islands.
Climate
In the coastal areas the climate is rather mild, but the hin -
terland knows a cold and inhospitable dark season, when life 
seems almost to come to a standstill.
Travel
The easiest way to travel in Saxony is to navigate along 
rivers, lakes, or the coast. Riding through light forests is slow -
er, because of the numerous impenetrable marshes and dense 
forests which dot the landscape. There are no bridges across 
the wild rivers, which have to be forded, preferably on horse -
back or in carts.
The seafaring Frisians have many merchant and fishing 
boats. They use smaller flatboats to cross the marshy plains on 
the mainland.

Each tribe is divided into independent clans. Each individu-

Edhilingui are essentially rich freemen who possess a 
wooden stronghold, often on a hilltop. They pay warriors 
(Hearthguards) in their service. The word “Saxon” means “war-
rior of a war-band” (much like the word Viking). Scops, respect-
ed harpists (a sort of bard, like the Danish skalds), are the living 
memory of the tribe and live either at their chief ’s court or wan-

Land cannot be held as a private property, so free Saxons 
can settle wherever they like. There is no central authority 
with which the Franks can negotiate lasting treaties, for the 
Saxons are divided into numerous autonomous clans. Clan 
leaders gather every year at the Allthing, somewhere in the 
forest near Marklo. Still, internal tribal cohesion is often 
weak due to frequent internal struggles between the freemen 
and the rich nobles. The latter sometimes gravitate towards 
the Franks, because they need alliances against each other 
and against the local freemen, who are often eager to revolt 
against their leaders. So Saxon nobles are often pro-Frankish 
and convert more easily, seeing the benefits of Christianity 
and feudalism, while the opposite is true for the frilingi, who 
are often afraid of their leader converting to Christianity and 
forcing them to do the same, consequently revolting against 
their own chiefs.
Religion
Their main gods are W otan (magic, knowledge), Donar 
(war, home) and Saxnot (the dead). They pray near shrines, 
temples and idols, but most of all to holy trees, like the Irminsul, 
which are used for blood sacrifices (mostly slaves and prisoners 
of war), and rich votive offerings which they bury under the 
roots. They ask their gods for help and their shamans or scops 
practice rituals of divination and soothsaying.
Saxons cremate their dead together with personal belong -
ings (swords, horses, and gold). Since the dead are sacred, no 
Saxon will ever take something from a Saxon burial mound.
Under Charles Martel, the chief missionary was Boniface,

Daily Life and Economy
Saxons are a semi-nomadic people and have no cities. Their 
villages are isolated from each other by forests and valleys, which 
are partly covered with peat bogs. Saxons depend on agriculture 
and the rearing of horses. The grassy valleys are used for cattle 
grazing. Most temporary settlements can be found along the rivers 
Elbe and W eser, where the Saxons live in wooden stable-houses. 
Whenever food shortages occur, the men raid neighboring re -
gions, usually the agriculturally more developed Franks or Slavs.
The Frisians live in the coastal marshes, either in small har-
bor villages with a long street parallel to the coast or navigable 
waterway, or on artificial, flat-topped clay mounds. Their wick -
erwork houses are daubed with clay to make them waterproof. 
They fish, raise cattle, horses and sheep on the nearby salt marsh-
es, and grow grain on the higher marsh land.
Saxons speak a Germanic language very similar to Frankish. 
They often give alliterative names to their children. Most Saxons 
are illiterate; only their shamans use runes to carve their secret 
formulas on stones or on pieces of wood, which they strap to 
a man’s arm with leather. Runes are mainly used as safeguards 
against illness, dangers and bad luck.
Saxony is poorly developed and the economy is based on 
cattle breeding rather than on arable farming. Since to Saxons 
land cannot be individually owned, a man’s wealth is measured 
by his livestock. Instead of coins, they use small pieces of hacked 
silver like the Danes. Nobles trade by bartering agricultural pro-
duce or luxury items like swords, jewelry or linen gowns. The 
only things they produce for trade are salt and ore. Frankish mer-
chants almost never venture into Saxony.
In contrast to inland Saxons, the Frisians have a coin-based 
currency system. They are renowned merchants (or pirates), and 
possess a considerable fleet based at Dorestad harbor. They trade 
widely with the British Isles, Scandinavia, the Baltic and along 
the rivers into the mainland all the way down to Spain and Italy.
Warfare
The Saxons fight almost exclusively as infantry, because the 
dense forests doesn’t really favor mounted combat. Saxons often use 
shield walls, and they dig hidden ditches to disrupt enemy cavalry.

Society
The Slavs are divided into patriarchal clans, linked into five 
different tribes: the Sorbs, the Obodrites, the Wilzi, the Czechs, 
and the Croats. There is little to no central power, as the tribes 
are as much rivals as a single people. The local chiefs dwell in cir-
cular wooden citadels, surrounded by open farming settlements. 
In their fortified homes, these nobles keep a warrior retinue 
called a druzhina. Their main function is to collect tribute and 
punish those who oppose their leader. The smaller settlements 
are governed by the tribe elders.
Religion
The pagan Slavs worship a polytheistic pantheon, including 
such deities as Perun (the main god, of storms, lightning and oak 
trees), Svarog (the sun, war, fertility), Stribog (hunting, winds), 
Khors (cattle) and Mokosh (women, the household). The Slavs 
pray in open-air cult places with a stone hearth and wooden stat-
ues on high river banks, surrounded by a ring ditch.
Nobles are interred in underground catacombs inside hills, 
while commoners are usually cremated on funeral pyres. When a 
chief dies, his family and serfs are also killed and cremated.
Slavs believe that spirits and demons live near crossroads, 
rivers, springs, trees, rocks or on the edges of bogs and ponds, as 
well as deep inside forests. The Slavs make offerings or animal 
sacrifices to keep them at bay. Friendly home spirits, like ances -
tors, help the priests by predicting the future, while malevolent 
demons (small, naked, dark-skinned creatures with extra fingers) 
have the ability to light fires, become invisible and cause illness.
Vila are strong, beautiful, naked nymphs or faeries armed 
with bows, who live near springs, in caves or under trees or rocks. 
They dance on hilltops at night and are able to transform into 
swans, snakes, horses, wolves or whirlwinds. The Faerie King 
lives in the deep forest near a sacred oak. He is generally hostile 
to humans and sometimes takes on the form of a giant wolf, owl 
or whirlwind.
Under Frankish military pressure, some tribes are forced to 
convert to Christianity.
Daily Life and Economy
Like the neighboring Saxons, the Slavs have no real cities. 
They live primarily as fishermen, cattle-raising herdsmen, or 
farmers, and trade with Saxons, Franks and Byzantines along 
the great navigable rivers and along the coasts. Their armed 
merchants travel in groups and use hacksilver, pelts and pieces of 
cloth as means of payment. In the areas under Hunnic control, 
the Slavs pay silver and furs as a tribute to their overlords.
They build their villages on steep promontories or in boggy 
places difficult to access. Undefended rural settlements are often 
near navigable rivers or a lake, giving villagers a way to escape in 
case of attack. Larger trading villages are surrounded by timber 
walls, earthen ramparts and ditches, with several entrances.
All men are shaved bald except for a single thin tail at the 
back. For women, hairstyle indicates their social position, age 
and marital status. Nobles practice polygamy. Chiefs welcome 
their important guests by taking a sauna together.
Warfare
Internal strife, small-scale raids and ambushes are daily 
bread to the Slavs. A typical warrior band consists of around 200 
warriors. They raid primarily to enhance wealth and community 
prestige for the leader and his druzhina, rather than to conquer.
Slavs usually use ambushes, sudden attacks and tricks, often 
during the night. They tend not to fight in an organized fashion, 
nor do they like to fight in the open, but rather prefer difficult 
swampy grounds where heavy armored knights are at a disadvan-
tage. Success against the Slavs depends mainly on speed, denying 
the targeted tribe time to organize its defenses.
Their lack of military unity and strength makes them 
easy victims. Slav prisoners are often used as slave laborers by 
the Frankish army, deported into Frankland, or sold. Slavs 
themselves do not take slaves, but they do take war prisoners. 
However, they do not keep these indefinitely, but let them go 
after a certain period of time and payment of ransom.

between forests and marshes for archers to cover a hasty retreat.

Society
Asturias is a pseudo-feudal kingdom, with allodial fiefs 
only. This means that few of the landed knights owe vassal ser -
vice to their king, apart from when the peace of the kingdom 
is threatened. The king cannot, therefore, undertake any expan-
sionist military campaigns and must content himself with the 
defense of the land.
The population is generally divided into three categories: 
rich landholders (nobles), merchants, and serfs. Nobles live in 
fortified manors (villas), or sometimes in small motte castles.
In Septimania too, many Visigothic nobles hold allodial 
lands as exclusive lifetime gifts, rather than as normal fiefs. Their 
ancestors were granted the land in full ownership in the time 
of Charles Martel and Pepin the Short. Local Visigothic mag -
nates sometimes give land as a precarial gift to other nobles or 
wealthy merchants, who may then use it as an allod with certain 
constraints.
Another system of landholding comes into being in 
Septimania and Provence, and later in the Spanish March: the 
so-called aprisio. Newly-cleared land is held by recently founded 
monasteries or Christian refugee settlers. Landowners holding 
an aprisio are always free men. They do homage directly to the 
emperor or his representatives, their community has the right to 
speak justice autonomously, and they do not pay taxes to a count 
nor even to the emperor. They still owe military service to the 
Emperor.
Land conquered from the Moors automatically becomes 
the property of the Crown, but Charlemagne or Louis often 
grant it to loyal nobles.
In Visigothic society, money is often seen as more import -
ant than noble descent, which leads to a certain class fluidity to 
the detriment of strictly feudal relations. 
Religion
Visigoths are Christians, including Mozarabs (Arabic-
speaking Christians) living in the Emirate of Cordoba, since 
their faith is strong enough to resist the temptations of the 
Saracen pagan worship. However, the Adoptionist heresy be -
comes very popular among the Visigoths, despite the work of 
Frankish missionary bishops and abbots.
After 778, many Visigothic refugees from the Emirate of 
Cordoba arrive in Septimania. Charlemagne encourages their 
return and founds many Benedictine monasteries as places of 
economic rebirth and protection for the local population. These 
religious centers counter the spread of Visigothic heresy.
Daily Life and Economy
The Visigothic territories were once rich farming lands 
producing wine and olive oil. The towns were autonomous and 
many Jews lived there in peace. But, at the start of Charlemagne’s 
reign, the lands of the Visigoths have been devastated by many 
years of warfare. T o recolonize these deserted lands, the king 
grants lands and immunities to Visigothic refugees on the con -
dition that they resettle their ancient homes. These are usually 
small agrarian settlements and, rarely, towns. 
Normally, inherited land is partitioned between all adult 
sons by drawing lots, but Visigothic tradition allows married 
women to hold and to inherit property independently from 
their husbands. Visigothic women thus have a relatively import-
ant public role, all the more so since social structures are based 
on monetary as well as territorial wealth. In the towns, mer -
chants and artisans have as much a say in local politics as knights.
The harbor towns actively trade with cities in Italy, Spain, 
Frankland and the British Isles. A sizable Jewish merchant com-
munity can be found in most Visigothic towns. In the country -
side, economic life is primarily local and centered around the 
rural villas. Sheep herding is the prime source of income in the 
mountains.
Warfare
The Visigoths of Septimania and Provence are renowned 
horsemen using repeated hit-and-run tactics. They frequently 
serve as mercenaries in feuds.
