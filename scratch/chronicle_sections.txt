1640: Charles Martel
1641: King Thierry IV (Theuderic) “reigned” from 721 to his death
1642: in 737, and he was not succeeded by anyone until Chilperic III
1643: (the last Merovingian king) in 743, who was deposed in 751,

1645: All the while, the real power was in the hands of Charles
1646: Martel, the Mayor of the Palace and Duke of the Franks. As
1647: such, he was in command of the royal army.
1648: Charles Martel was a tough and talented military com -
1649: mander, who celebrated many victories against the Saxons,
1650: Moors and other enemies. He is most famous for defeating
1651: the Moors at the Battle of Poitiers (or Battle of T ours) in 732.
1652: As the de facto ruler of the Franks, he laid out the basis for
1653: the concept of knighthood, and forged a solid alliance with
1654: the Pope.
1655: Pepin the Short
1656: Son of Charles Martel, he succeeded his father as Mayor of
1657: the Palace, but later he deposed King Chilperic III and was
1658: anointed King of the Franks in 751.
1659: During his political and military career, he had many
1660: problems with his brother Carloman and half-brother Grifo.
1661: As a king, Pepin waged many offensive wars in which he
1662: forced the submission of the southern duchies of Burgundy,
1663: Aquitaine, Provence and Septimania.
1664: King Pepin confirmed the pre-eminence of the Frankish
1665: people in Western Europe. Inside his realm he promoted feu -
1666: dalism, vassalage and the use of knights as heavy cavalry.

3320: Chapter Three: Personality:
3321: Traits and Passions
3322: C
3323: harlemagne’s epics are stories of personality and action
3324: on a grand scale. Everyone whose name is remembered
3325: is known for both great deeds and great feelings. No
3326: great character is a mere two-dimensional cutout whose history
3327: consists solely of high scores and lists of events. Everyone feels
3328: strongly about something.
3329: Personality in P aladin  is depicted through traits and pas -
3330: sions. They provide guidelines for playing characters who may
3331: be quite different from the player.
3332: Why Do We Need
3333: Personality Values?
3334: T
3335: raits and passions are used to quantify character behav -
3336: ior. They are used to make the player’s roleplaying task
3337: easier by providing guidelines for how the character acts,
3338: has acted, and is likely to continue to act. Numerical values are
3339: assigned to each trait and passion, and rolls are made using the
3340: values to determine behavior.
3341: Consistency
3342: In cases of distinctive behavior, your character may have to
3343: struggle to maintain his behavior, especially in the face of con -
3344: flicting desires. The chaste knight is able to maintain his chastity,
3345: the drunkard remains a drunkard, and the courageous will be
3346: courageous — sometimes even against his better judgment.
3347: Even after a long hiatus from play, you’ll be able to remem-
3348: ber how a given character acts just by glancing at his traits and
3349: passions.
3350: Entertainment Value
3351: The interplay of character personalities provides a great
3352: deal of the fun in P aladin . With traits and passions, each char-
3353: acter’s personality is very different. A character may chivalrous
3354: like Roland, wise like Oliver, or a sinister knight with a treach -
3355: erous side, like Ganelon. Each character has a well-defined and
3356: colorful basis for roleplaying and for action within the game.
3357: T raits and passions are entertaining because they can lead
3358: to amusing results. In addition, some successful trait or passion
3359: rolls result in the character becoming inspired, with temporary
3360: benefits to die rolls. Fumbled passion rolls might even result in
3361: madness.
3362: Glory
3363: Any dramatic pattern of behavior gains Glory. Characters
3364: with interesting personalities generally gain more Glory than
3365: those without. However, behavior in accord with society’s ideals
3366: gains more Glory than idiosyncrasies.
3367: Authenticity
3368: P aladin  deals with what is indisputably the greatest of all
3369: medieval stories—the epic story of Charlemagne and his pala -
3370: dins. The game takes place in a world of knights and ladies, in an
3371: age of feudalism and chivalry, where democracy is seditious and
3372: unequal authoritarianism the norm.
3373: Players control the actions of characters that may be, quite
3374: understandably, unlike themselves, yet they are expected to act
3375: appropriately to their station. Acts such as incidental murder,
3376: stealing horses, participating in ambushes, or unmerciful behav-
3377: ior are not necessarily villainous; for death, slaughter, and misery
3378: are normal parts of a knight’s life.
3379: How, then, can you know what is appropriate and correct?
3380: T raits and passions are the primary indicators that you use to de-
3381: termine “correctness” in the P aladin  setting. If your character
3382: gains the “proper” traits and passions, then he is doing just exact-
3383: ly those things that constitute correct behavior in Carolingian
3384: society.
3385: If you wish to run a character who defies Carolingian tra -
3386: ditions, you can and may, but your character will probably lose
3387: out in various passion values for acting outside the norms of
3388: society. The Gamemaster must decide whether a loss is war -
3389: ranted and how much the loss should be, based on the specific
3390: circumstances.

3413: Trait and Passion Rolls
3414: During play, character behavior is often challenged by
3415: the Gamemaster. T emptations are paraded forth, moral crises
3416: heaped up, and critical judgments and actions must be made.
3417: Since traits and passions define the character’s personality,
3418: they must be consulted whenever the Gamemaster determines
3419: that a critical decision is at hand. In crises, individuals act ac -
3420: cording to character, not deliberate choice. Custom and train -
3421: ing triumph over intellect. Players may not want their charac -
3422: ters to do something dictated by a die roll, but free choice is
3423: not always possible.
3424: Ordinary Traits and Passions
3425: T raits and passions with values of 15 or less do not have to
3426: be rolled against if the player wishes to use his personal will to
3427: determine an action, although rolling is obviously the most im -
3428: partial way to determine action.
3429: Characters who consistently act a certain way will eventual-
3430: ly have traits or passions valued at 16 or higher, due to the rules
3431: below, at which time they’ll have to make the required rolls.
3432: Notable Traits and Passions
3433: T raits and passions greater than 15 are noted by society and
3434: gain Glory, and such traits or passions must be tested with a die
3435: roll when character behavior is challenged in a crisis. Basically, if
3436: you get Glory for a trait or passion, rolls based on the value must
3437: be made when required by the Gamemaster.
3438: This does not mean that trait rolls must be used whenever
3439: the character makes any decision in the game. Even characters
3440: with famous characteristics are allowed free choice of behavior
3441: except when the plot demands otherwise. The Gamemaster
3442: should request trait rolls only when a trait is tested in an
3443: important situation. In general, trait rolls simulate situations in
3444: which a crisis forces the character to act unconsciously.
3445: Modifiers
3446: T rait or passion values may be modified based on the situ-
3447: ation in order to keep the game realistic. Players are responsible
3448: for reminding the Gamemaster when a situation might call for a
3449: trait or passion modifier. The Gamemaster determines whether
3450: such modifiers are valid.
3451: Changes in Value
3452: V alues for personality scores rise and fall during the game.
3453: Experience checks for traits and passions are assigned by the
3454: Gamemaster in a slightly different fashion from checks for
3455: skills. Players who are reluctant to roll should not be forced to.
3456: However, if they consistently have their characters act against
3457: their established personalities, the Gamemaster should have
3458: them check the trait or passion they actually exhibited, in order
3459: to simulate the possibility of a change in the character’s psychol-
3460: ogy and reputation.
3461: For example, the player of a knight with a Generous value
3462: of 13 loots and pillages his foes at every turn and constantly talks
3463: about the amount of money (or lack thereof ) his character has.
3464: This attitude and behavior is not in keeping with the Generous
3465: trait, so the Gamemaster is perfectly within his rights to tell the
3466: character to check his Selfish trait the next time he expresses the
3467: desire to loot an enemy.
3468: Passions are particularly vulnerable to reduction. When a
3469: character acts against a passion, no check is assigned; instead the
3470: Gamemaster simply instructs the player to reduce the value of
3471: the passion by one point immediately. Any failed passion roll
3472: causes the character to lose a point.
3473: Acting consistently, according to the role of your charac -
3474: ter, will prevent compulsory checks and reductions from being
3475: necessary.
3476: The traits and passions system should not to be used to turn
3477: the Player-knights into puppets. Most of the time characters just
3478: do whatever the player wishes them to do, collecting checks
3479: along the way. However, if your character has a reputation, it’s
3480: only fair that he maintains it or loses it. The system ensures that
3481: this occurs.
3482: Evil or Undesirable Behavior
3483: It is not in the spirit of the game for Player-knights to
3484: become evil knights. However, minor character flaws such as
3485: Cruelty or Laziness can be very amusing in moderation, and
3486: give the Gamemaster more opportunities to create interesting
3487: situations.
3488: With one or two undesirable traits or inferior passion val -
3489: ues, characters can have weaknesses other than those revealed
3490: only in mortal combat, giving the Gamemaster the possibility of
3491: creating non-lethal challenges for characters. Given the dangers
3492: involved in combat, this is a useful opportunity.
